# The Confessions of Al Ghazzali

*A Chronicle of Light summary of The Confessions of Al Ghazzali by Al-Ghazali, translated by Claud Field*

## In one paragraph
This short spiritual autobiography, written in answer to a brother in the faith, recounts how Al Ghazzali, a celebrated professor of theology at Bagdad, searched for certain truth amid the many sects of his age. He tests scholastic theology, philosophy and Sufism in turn, falls into a two-month scepticism, is healed by a "light" God casts into his heart, abandons his post, and finds in Sufi practice and the reality of inspiration the certitude that argument could not give. It is for readers of Islamic intellectual and religious history, and for anyone interested in the rare genre of Eastern autobiography.

## Core argument
Ghazzali begins from the problem of diversity: the sects are "a deep ocean strewn with shipwrecks," each sure of its own salvation. Observing that children simply inherit their parents' creeds, he sets out to find what certitude really is. He defines it as knowledge so clear that no doubt or error can enter, and which would stand even against a miracle-worker who claimed three is more than ten.

Applying that test, he finds that only sense-perceptions and necessary principles seem to qualify, and then undermines both. The senses mislead (the apparently still shadow, the star that looks as small as a piece of gold), and reason itself might be judged by a higher faculty, as waking life judges dreams. After about two months as a thoroughgoing sceptic he was restored not by proofs but by a light God placed in his heart.

He then surveys the three groups of seekers. Scholastic theology defends orthodoxy but rests on premises it shares with its opponents, so it could not cure him. Philosophy, which he mastered from books in about two years, is divided into mathematics, logic, physics, metaphysics, politics and moral philosophy. Its metaphysics, following Aristotle through Farabi and Avicenna, contains errors he reduces to twenty propositions: three irreligious, seventeen heretical. Yet he insists that truth should be judged by its content, not by whoever speaks it, and that the learned should extract the good from the bad as a skilled assayer does with a false coiner's purse.

Sufism alone supplies what he lacked, because it consists in experience rather than definition, as knowing what drunkenness is differs from being drunk. Seeing that his teaching was driven by desire for reputation, he struggled for six months from Rajab 1096, lost the power to speak, and left Bagdad for Syria, Jerusalem and the Hedjāz. After ten years of intermittent devotion he concluded that the Sufis are the true pioneers on the path of God.

He closes by arguing that inspiration is a real faculty above reason, as reason is above sense and discrimination. Sleep gives a glimpse of it. Its reality is confirmed by experience, not by isolated miracles. He also answers the objection that the bad example of learned men discredits religion.

## Key themes
### Certitude and the limits of reason
Certitude must be proof against all doubt. Ghazzali shows that sense and even reason can be called into question, and that final assurance comes through divine light rather than a "concatenation of proofs."

### Judging truth by its content, not its source
Quoting Ali, "Do not seek for the truth by means of men; find first the truth and then you will recognise those who follow it," he argues that truth does not become false because an impostor or philosopher states it. Gold, honey and antidote stay pure despite their surroundings.

### The dangers of philosophy and of its crude opponents
Mathematics and logic are harmless in themselves, but they tempt students to trust philosophers on religion. The ignorant defender who denies the exact sciences, such as eclipse calculations, harms Islam equally by making it appear to rest on ignorance.

### Knowledge versus experience
Sufism cannot be learned from books. Theory about renouncement, health or drunkenness is different from living it. He names three degrees: knowledge (certitude from proof), transport (the state itself), and faith (believing others' experience).

### Worldly attachment and conversion
He honestly examines his motives and finds his lecturing driven by vanity. His six months of paralysing indecision, the loss of speech, and his departure from Bagdad portray conversion as a costly struggle against honours, wealth and family ties.

### Inspiration as a higher faculty
Human faculties unfold in stages: touch, sight and the other senses, discrimination at about seven, reason, and then prophetic inspiration. Those who deny it resemble a man blind from birth denying colours.

### The Sufis and the Prophet
Ghazzali presents Sufi purification, from emptying the heart of all but God to being "lost in God," as the path closest to the Prophet's own retreat at Mount Hira. He rejects the terms haloul, ittihād and wasl for the final union.

## Chapter-by-chapter
- **Introduction**: The translator gives Ghazzali's birth at Tus in A.D. 1058, his Sufi guardianship, his Bagdad professorship, his retreat and pilgrimage, and his death in A.D. 1111, with brother Ahmad's account of it. He compares the work to Newman and Bunyan and cites Macdonald's high estimate of Ghazzali.
- **Ghazzali's Search for Truth**: Answering a brother's questions, he describes the sea of sects, his early break with inherited belief, and his definition of certitude as knowledge that no miracle could shake.
- **The Subterfuges of the Sophists**: He finds sense-perception and then reason doubtful, using shadows, stars and dreams. After about two months of scepticism, God heals him with a light in the heart.
- **The Different Kinds of Seekers after Truth**: He sorts seekers into scholastic theologians, philosophers and Sufis, and resolves to study them in that order.
- **The Aim of Scholastic Theology and its Results**: Theology defends orthodoxy against heretics but relies on accepted authority, so it could not satisfy him. He then turns to philosophy, mastering it in two years of private study while teaching three hundred students at Bagdad.
- **Concerning the Philosophical Sects and the Stigma of Infidelity which attaches to them all**: He classes philosophers as Materialists, Naturalists and Theists. He counts Socrates, Plato, Aristotle, Avicenna and Farabi among the last, and still regards them as unbelievers.
- **Divisions of the Philosophic Sciences**: He assesses six sciences. Mathematics and logic are harmless but risky in their effects, physics is acceptable if nature is God's instrument, and metaphysics is the chief source of error. Political science and moral philosophy borrow from revelation and the Sufis. He then defends extracting truth from tainted sources while warning the unprepared away from philosophic books.
- **Sufism**: He studies Sufi writings, sees that the goal needs practice and ecstasy, and examines his own worldly motives. After his struggle and loss of speech he leaves Bagdad, travels to Damascus, Jerusalem and Mecca and spends ten years in intermittent devotion. He concludes that Sufi life is unsurpassed.
- **The Reality of Inspiration: its Importance for the Human Race**: He argues that inspiration is a higher faculty, illustrated by dreams and by medicine and astronomy, and that Muhammed's prophethood is verified through experience of his teaching. He ends by answering unbelief caused by philosophy and by the bad example of learned men, and with a prayer.

## People, places and dates
- **Tus, Khorassan, A.D. 1058**: Ghazzali's birthplace and birth year (Introduction).
- **Bagdad**: site of his professorship, which he left (Introduction; Sufism).
- **Nishapur**: where he later accepted a similar post and the birthplace of Omar Khayyām, whom he reportedly met and disliked (Introduction; Search for Truth).
- **A.D. 1111**: his death at Tus, with his brother Ahmad's account (Introduction).
- **Rajab, A.D. 1096**: the start of his six months of indecision before leaving Bagdad (Sufism).
- **Damascus, Jerusalem (Sanctuary of the Rock), Mecca, Medina and the Hedjāz**: stations of his retreat and pilgrimage (Sufism).
- **Mount Hira**: where the Prophet retired before his commission (Sufism).
- **Abu Talib of Mecca, Hareth el Muhasibi, Junaid, Shibli, Abu Yezid Bustami**: Sufi authorities he studied (Sufism).
- **Socrates, Plato, Aristotle, Farabi, Avicenna (Ibn Sina)**: philosophers discussed (Philosophical Sects).
- **Ali, son of Abu Talib**: source of the maxim on judging truth by itself (Divisions of the Philosophic Sciences).
- **The Ta'limites, Mutazilites, Ibahat, Brothers of Purity**: groups and works he addresses (various sections).
- **His other works**: Revivification of the Sciences of Religion, Destruction of the Philosophers, Criteria of the differences which divide Islam from Atheism, The Chief Aim, Marvels of the Heart, The Just Balance, Alchemy of Happiness (Introduction and later sections).
- **Claud Field, Macdonald, Whinfield, Browne**: the translator and scholars cited in the Introduction.

## Takeaways
- Ghazzali defines certitude as knowledge that leaves no room for doubt and cannot be shaken even by a miracle.
- He shows that senses and reason can both be doubted, using shadows, stars and the dream analogy.
- His recovery from about two months of scepticism came through divine light, not argument.
- Scholastic theology defends orthodoxy but depends on premises accepted from authority, so it could not give him certainty.
- He mastered philosophy from books alone in about two years before judging it, holding that no one can refute what they do not understand.
- He reduces the philosophers' errors to twenty propositions, three of them irreligious: denial of bodily resurrection, of God's knowledge of particulars, and the eternity of the world.
- Truth should be judged by its content rather than by who says it, and a wise person extracts the good from the bad.
- Mathematics and logic are neutral for religion, but admiration for them can lead students to trust philosophers on religious matters.
- Sufism is learned through experience and practice, not definition, and requires renouncing honour, wealth and attachments.
- Ghazzali admits his own teaching was motivated by love of reputation, and that this realisation led to his departure from Bagdad.
- Inspiration is a faculty above reason, partly glimpsed in dreams, and its truth is confirmed through verified experience rather than isolated miracles.
- Learned men's sins do not disprove religion, and true knowledge increases fear of God and guards against persistent sin.

## Notable passages
> Know then, my brothers (may God direct you in the right way), that the diversity in beliefs and religions, and the variety of doctrines and sects which divide men, are like a deep ocean strewn with shipwrecks, from which very few escape safe and sound.

> I owed my deliverance, not to a concatenation of proofs and arguments, but to the light which God caused to penetrate into my heart—the light which illuminates the threshold of all knowledge.

> In every case they judge of the truth according to its professors and not of men according to the truth which they profess, a _ne plus ultra_ of error.

> I saw that Sufism consists in experiences rather than in definitions, and that what I was lacking belonged to the domain, not of instruction, but of ecstasy and initiation.

> Thus I remained, torn asunder by the opposite forces of earthly passions and religious aspirations, for about six months from the month Rajab of the year A.D. 1096.

> In the same way the contact of truth with falsehood does not change truth into falsehood, any more than it changes falsehood into truth.
